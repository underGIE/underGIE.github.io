01

PARTYAH

Live demoReact Native

An Israeli event marketplace. Organisers book DJs, DJs manage gigs, guests walk in with a QR ticket. Hebrew-first, right-to-left, mobile.

Pre-launch on a live production backend. Every core flow was walked end to end against the real database and logged: phone login, a seven-step event wizard, DJ search and booking, notifications fired by database triggers, door-mode QR scanning, and a public Party Page you can send in a WhatsApp message.

  • 726 tests, 47 suites, re-run the day this page went up
  • 29 migrations applied to a live PostgreSQL 17
  • Row-level security on all 23 tables, advisors driven to zero
  • CI gate: typecheck, lint, tests and a gitleaks secret scan
  • Scheduled jobs, rate limiting, and an audit trail of every backend action
  • Money never guessed: stored in agorot, one integer, no floats

My role: I set the nine rules this codebase obeys, including money as integers and row-level security on every table, and sent back everything that broke one.

02

Shinobi Blast

Play in browserGodot

I wanted to understand what actually keeps people inside a game like Clash Royale or Castle Busters. So I am building one and measuring it.

The build is the instrument. To ask how a player gets pulled forward, I built the layer that does the pulling: a nine-rank trophy road where the stakes scale to the opponent, daily quests, win chests, a seven-day check-in, a season pass. To ask whether the match underneath holds up, I built a seeded AI-versus-AI harness that reports distributions, and a twenty-match playtest gate I have to pass myself before anything ships.

  • Godot 4.3, 73 headless test suites, playable in a browser today
  • The harness reports distributions, never a single run: after an n=10 "collapse" turned out to be noise, the rule became n≥30 for core metrics
  • A measurement rewrote the design: stamping every shot with its target found zero core-aimed shots out of 783
  • "It feels stuck" became a number: 47 freezes in a 217-second recording, 75% of the match a still image
  • Economy decided before it was coded: three currencies, build timers, and no energy gate on the aim loop
  • Honest about what it is not: solo and hotseat only, no netcode yet, and the store is a labelled sandbox

My role: I write the specs, red-pen my own design before it gets built, run the playtests, and decide what ships. Still building it, and I intend to publish it.

My role: all of it, from interviewing the person it was made for to the polish pass. It is also my prototype for a personalised-game service: one engine, many theme packs.

04

Tabim

iOS · AudioIn build

A guitar teacher that listens. The microphone judges what you actually played, note by note, in time.

The hard part is not the game around it, it is honesty. A score follower warps the clock to the player instead of demanding the player follow a metronome. A calibration routine measures the device's own latency and repairs itself when it stores nonsense. When a chord is wrong the app says which finger, not just "try again": gold rings are the chord it asked for, blue dots are what it heard you play.

  • Swift with a C++ audio core, pitch and onset detection on device
  • 602 tests re-run today: 246 XCTest plus 356 swift-testing cases in 43 suites
  • A warped clock: the score follower re-times itself to the player rather than making the player chase a metronome
  • A "detection is broken" bug traced to poisoned calibration data, then made self-healing
  • Six per-string level meters, so a silent D string is diagnosed rather than just marked wrong
  • I am the test subject: I record the takes and write the verdicts

My role: product owner and the guitarist. Every sprint starts from a device-test verdict I wrote after playing it myself.

Mid-riff, with the live latency and microphone readouts · mastery and trend for each of eight chords · the song library. Captured from the running app.

05

The Farm

ConsultingDelivered

I worked the branches myself before I proposed anything. The farm had grown for years without a planning layer, and I wanted to see an ordinary day before I drew one.

I mapped the branches and how work moves between them, ran the requirements process with the farm head and the teams, and turned it into a diagnosis dashboard for management. Then I built the planning instrument: a work-plan template for the teaching staff, one sheet per school month, where every activity is broken into the same six lines.

  • Diagnosis dashboard: the operating model, the process maps, an impact-versus-effort matrix
  • 15-tab work-plan template: a sheet per school month, an action catalogue, an annual summary that builds itself
  • Six lines per activity: action, description, quantity, target, people, cost
  • Designed to consolidate: each staff member fills their own copy, the copies roll up to the manager's master
  • Monday.com against a custom build, both priced, one staged recommendation
  • A written methodology so the next engagement starts further along

My role: technology and process consultant, part time, alongside my degree. The lesson I kept: the tooling has to follow the process, especially where the staff are not technical.

06

National Programme Budget

Ministry of HealthAwaiting sign-off

A national health programme ran its budget across five spreadsheets and seventeen tabs. Ask how much was planned in 2024 and you got four different answers.

I read every file and mapped the six stations the money passes through: plan, request, approval, contract, execution, report. Each station had quietly grown its own file. The replacement is one workbook where a budget line moves through four money states, tasks count down their own deadlines, and a journal records what was decided and by whom, so the next person in the chair can read the history instead of guessing at it.

  • Eight tabs, one source of truth, and a dashboard nobody types into
  • Four money states: planned, approved, committed, paid
  • The number that matters: money committed but not yet paid, the exposure nobody was tracking
  • Reports specified as views, not new files: one export per stakeholder, generated from the same rows
  • Written for a non-technical owner: plain-language spec, flowchart, simulation guide, and a walkthrough script to read aloud
  • Staged rollout: skeleton, sign-off, a pilot year, then migrate the history

My role: digital and technology lead on a three-person team, a scholarship position. My counterpart runs a national programme and is not a technical person, which is the whole design brief: the system is only real once the person who owns it trusts it.

Work the job first

The Farm: I worked the branches myself before mapping a single process.
02

Write the spec

Plain language, decisions listed, open questions named. A document the least technical person in the room can argue with.

The budget system: the spec opens by saying it is a proposal, not a decision.
03

Direct, review, verify

The keystrokes are mostly an AI's. The architecture, the review and the responsibility are mine, and nothing lands until I can show it working.

Everywhere: a claim is only done once a test, a screenshot or a database query backs it.

Measure, then decide
